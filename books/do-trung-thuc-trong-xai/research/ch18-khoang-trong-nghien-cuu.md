## The table-of-contents re-check: a sixth mode

The open item that ran from decision 56 closed at chapter 17 with a final score
of one survivor in nine. **Chapter 18's line was never in that set.** It is the
tenth init-written line and the last one in the book, so this session runs the
five-leg check on it.

The line, as initialized and as it still stood at the start of this session:

> 18. **Khoảng trống nghiên cứu** - synthesis: the intersection of the
> limitation and future-work statements of papers 21-26; what a validated
> instrument would have to look like.

Decision 10 records where it came from: "which is the corpus README's own
prescription". Verified, and the transcription is faithful. The corpus README at
`F:\repo\thesis-xai-faithfulness\README.md`, under the heading **Where the
research gap lives**, reads:

> The Tier B papers (21-26) collectively show the field lacks agreed ground
> truth for 'faithful'. The escape routes the critique literature points to:
> (a) known-mechanism models where ground truth is constructed, and (b) human
> studies. Beware the failure mode of this topic: proposing yet another metric
> that is itself unvalidated. Keep a running log of every 'limitation' and
> 'future work' statement from papers 21-26 - their intersection is your
> research-gap statement.

The five legs, run in order:

1. **Does it promise more than the papers contain?** Yes, in the word
   `intersection`. An intersection is what is common to all the members, and the
   book's own log shows that three of the papers have nothing in common with the
   rest on this question. Taken literally the intersection over the named range
   is not the gap; it is empty as soon as paper 26 is in the range, because
   paper 26 names no missing instrument at all.
2. **Does it promise less than the papers contain?** Yes, in the range
   `papers 21-26`. The log is not six papers. Part IV runs to chapter 14, so the
   log the book actually filled covers papers 21 through 29, which is nine; and
   Part V's notes record limitation or future-work statements for six more that
   are deliberately not entries in that log - papers 30, 31 and 32 in chapter
   15's note, paper 18 in chapter 16's, and papers 16 and 08 in chapter 17's.
   **The line names six papers where fifteen have statements on the record.**
3. **Do the papers stand in the relation the line implies?** No. `Intersection`
   implies they all say the same thing about the same absence. Six of the nine
   Part IV papers do; three do not, and the three non-crossings differ from each
   other as much as they differ from the six.
4. **Does the line's organising predicate hold?** This is the same word again,
   and it is the sharpest reading of the failure. `Intersection` is not a noun
   the line uses about its papers; it is a **method** the line prescribes for
   getting the answer, and the material does not admit that method.
5. **Read against the chapters already drafted.** Section 9.7, section 16.7 and
   section 17.7 have each recorded a piece of the gap's shape that no
   intersection would produce: a question answered on one side and never asked
   on the other, three escape routes that form a loop rather than three exits,
   and a transfer whose direction runs the opposite way from the one the book's
   own thesis line implies.

**The sixth mode, and it is new.** The nine lines checked before this one failed
against their own papers (promising more, promising less, mis-arranging them,
asserting a predicate one of them refutes) or against the book (stating a
relation backwards). This one is different in kind: it is a faithful
transcription of a **prescription written before the reading happened**, and it
fails because the reading happened. The README's sentence was a plan for what
the log would look like; the log looks like something else.

**What makes this mode worth carrying forward past this book**: none of the five
legs catches it by itself, because the line is not false about any paper. It is
false about the *shape of the answer*, and the only thing that reveals that is
having done the work the line prescribes and looking at what came out. So the
cheapest guard is not a sixth leg but a habit: **a table-of-contents line that
names a method rather than a subject is a hypothesis about the material, and it
is re-checked when the material is in, not before drafting.**

## The five conditions on a future instrument

The second half of the TOC line, "what a validated instrument would have to look
like", is already assembled: five chapters each derived one condition and handed
it forward, and section 16.7 collects the first four at a general level. Read off
the pages this session:

1. **Describe where the two differ, rather than certify that they agree.**
   Section 13.6, from theorem `thm:ch13-khoang-cach-do-phuc-tap`. Because the
   theorem says in advance that a readable explanation of a complex model does
   not agree with it, the job left for an instrument is to say where the
   divergence sits in the input space, in which direction, and whether it falls
   in a region the user cares about. This one comes from a theorem rather than a
   measurement.
2. **Publish the intervention set the scores are relative to.** Section 14.7,
   from the scaffolding attack: an attribution method is faithful only relative
   to its own intervention set, and the attack works because nobody is obliged
   to say what that set is. None of chapter 14's three papers proposes the
   obligation, though all three exploit or repair the consequence of its absence.
3. **Report the forcing test beside the naming test.** Section 15.7: every
   reading of a name off an internal unit must come with a test that forces that
   unit, and both results reported, because the share of units where both hold
   is 18.84 percent.
4. **Say what validated the instrument this instrument is built on.** Section
   16.7, from the audit of route 3: an instrument standing on another instrument
   has to name what checked the one underneath. Route 3 and its own repair both
   fail this, and chapter 11 met a loop of the same shape one level up.
5. **Say which of the two questions it asks.** Section 17.7: whether it asks
   about the path the model actually took, or about the causal influence of the
   explanation on the answer. These are two rows of table 1.1, and chapter 17
   found three papers printing one name for both. This is the only one of the
   five that demands no extra publication; it demands the right name.

### Three papers that could have closed the gap, and did not

The two reading decisions chapter 18 owed produced a third paper, and all three
are read this session. **None refutes the book's claim; the first states it, the

#### 1. The meta-evaluation paper states the book's claim as its own premise

Hedström, Bommer, Wickstrøm, Samek, Lapuschkin and Höhne, *The Meta-Evaluation
Problem in Explainable AI: Identifying Reliable Estimators with MetaQuantus*,
arXiv:2302.07265, v1 2023-02-14, **v2 2023-07-19 is current and is what was
read**. Published in *Transactions on Machine Learning Research* 06/2023; arXiv
carries no `journal-ref`, and the venue is confirmed from the paper's own running
header on every page, which is the stronger source. **Printed folio matches the
PDF page index**; every page carries the TMLR header at the top and the number at
the foot, which is what made the anchor check below necessary.

This is the paper chapter 11 deferred. Paper 23 describes it as the
meta-evaluation problem \enquote{which quantifies attribution quality}, and it
does: its object is the faithfulness metrics of feature attribution, naming
Faithfulness Correlation and Pixel-Flipping among them. If any document were the
counterexample to section 9.7, this is it.

**It is the opposite of a counterexample.** Section 2.2, titled *The Challenge of
Unverifiability*, **p. 5**, read from the rendered page:

> Another key observation is that we cannot determine the accuracy or validity of
> an estimator (i.e., whether it actually measures the intended quality) since
> such an assessment requires access to ground truth labels. However, as
> reliability analysis does not depend on the availability of ground truth
> labels, it is still possible to study the reliability of an estimator, which
> refers to its overall consistency.

And section 3.1, *Defining Failure Modes*, **p. 6**, read from the rendered page:

> Without ground truth information, we cannot validate or optimise the quality
> estimators against what we want them to fulfil, but we can instead articulate
> edge-case scenarios or behaviours that we do not want them to exhibit.

Its abstract says the framework analyses noise resilience and reactivity to
randomness, \enquote{thus circumventing the need for ground truth labels}.

**The apparatus those two sentences sit inside**, recorded because review
question 9 sends the reader to it. Section 2.2, p. 5, draws the quality-estimation
process as a directed acyclic graph and splits its variables into **verifiable
spaces**, where ground truth labels are available, and **unverifiable spaces**,
where they are not; the explanation and the estimator's own output both fall in
the second. The paper names the consequence the *Challenge of Unverifiability*:
the evaluation function is applied to the output of an unverifiable function, so
the evaluation outcome is unverifiable too. Section 3.1, p. 6, then defines
Failure Mode 1 (Noise Resilience) and Failure Mode 2 (Adversary Reactivity). The
chapter does not print this apparatus, because its argument needs only the two
sentences; the review question asks the reader to fetch it, which is why it is
recorded here.

**An anchor correction made this session, and it is the reason the rule exists.**
The verification that produced these two quotes placed both on p. 5. The second
is on p. 6: the page break falls between them, and in this paper's layout the
folio sits at the foot while the venue line sits at the head of the *next* page,
so an extraction reads the two quotes as adjacent. Both were re-read as rendered
images and the anchors above are from the images.

**What the book takes from it.** Two things, and the second is the book's own
reading. First, an independent, peer-reviewed source on the feature-attribution
side states in 2023 that the validity of a faithfulness metric cannot be
determined for want of ground truth. That is section 9.7's claim, made by the
field rather than by this book, in the one place a refutation would have lived.
Second, **its response to that wall is the same move chapter 08 records the
metrics themselves making**: unable to check sufficiency, it formulates necessary
conditions - failure modes a good estimator must not exhibit - and tests those.
Section 8.6 says that about the randomization tests applied to explanations. This
is the identical move applied one level up, to the instruments, and for the
identical stated reason. The book names that rhyme; neither paper does.

#### 2. The shared reference of the critique core is a review, not an experiment

Nauta, Trienes, Pathak, Nguyen, Peters, Schmitt, Schlötterer, van Keulen and
Seifert, *From Anecdotal Evidence to Quantitative Evaluation Methods: A
Systematic Review on Evaluating Explainable AI*, arXiv:2201.08164, v3 2023-02-24,
*ACM Computing Surveys* 55(13s), DOI 10.1145/3583558, 2023-07-13. This matches
the citation the open item recorded from three of the four critique-core papers.

A systematic review: a DBLP query over 12 venues for 2014-2020 gives 606
candidates, 494 after exclusions, **361 included papers, of which 312 introduce a
new XAI method**; inter-reviewer agreement Cohen's kappa 0.625.

It reports **no experiment of its own**, and this is the answer to the reading
decision: the one reference shared across the critique core is a catalogue of
what other people do. Its Co-12 property *Correctness* is its name for
faithfulness, p. 15: \enquote{The correctness property addresses to what extent
the explanation is faithful to the predictive model it explains.} Its description
of the deletion family, p. 20: \enquote{The Single Deletion method evaluates the
change in output when removing or perturbing one feature and compares that with
the explanation's importance score. In a correct explanation, the explanation's
feature importance score should be proportional to the shift in output
distribution.} The criterion there is the model's own output shift, which is the
proxy chapter 08 spends a chapter on, described without being interrogated.

Its own forward-looking sentence, p. 28: \enquote{However, to reach this full
potential, XAI methods should be extensively validated in order to ensure that
they are reliable and useful.} **The object of the verb is the XAI method, not
the evaluation method**, and the review's own catalogue supplies the tools that
would do the validating. That is the whole distinction one level up, and the
book's terminology already separates the two.

**One number worth keeping and one not.** 312 of 361 included papers introduce a
new XAI method. That is a fact about a review's own corpus of evaluation-related
papers, and it must not be read as a validation rate; paper 24 owns the
validation count, 128 of 18,254, and the two are not comparable. Recorded here so
that a later session does not merge them.

#### 3. The benchmark that ran the comparison and read it as being about explainers

Liu, Khandagale, White and Neiswanger, *Synthetic Benchmarks for Scientific
Research in Explainable Machine Learning*, arXiv:2106.12543, submitted
2021-06-23, **v4 of 2021-11-04 is current and is what was read**; NeurIPS Datasets
and Benchmarks Track 2021. 25 pages. **Printed folio matches the PDF page index.**
This paper is not named by any corpus paper; it reached the book through Nauta's
reference list, and it is the one the reading decision above would have missed.

It releases XAI-Bench: synthetic datasets on which conditional expectations are
exact, plus five evaluation metrics. Two of the five are ones chapter 08 already
walked through and keyed, ROAR and infidelity. The other three are `faithfulness`
in Alvarez-Melis and Jaakkola's sense, `monotonicity`, and **`GT-Shapley`, which
is the Pearson correlation of the attribution weights with the exact Shapley
values**, p. 4.

**Table 1, printed p. 7, read from the rendered page.** A multilayer perceptron on
the Gaussian nonlinear-additive dataset at feature correlation 0.5, eight methods
including a RANDOM baseline that emits standard-normal weights, mean and standard
deviation over ten trials:

| metric | RANDOM | SHAP | SHAPR | LIME | MAPLE | L2X | breakDown |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| faithfulness (up) | 0.002 | 0.651 | **0.799** | 0.524 | 0.478 | 0.000 | 0.110 |
| monotonicity (up) | 0.525 | 0.537 | 0.550 | 0.517 | 0.543 | 0.535 | **0.562** |
| ROAR (up) | 0.380 | 0.455 | **0.465** | 0.432 | 0.432 | 0.365 | 0.329 |
| GT-Shapley (up) | 0.004 | 0.810 | **0.930** | 0.711 | 0.530 | -0.014 | -0.127 |
| infidelity (down) | 0.114 | 0.050 | 0.036 | 0.053 | **0.019** | 0.025 | 0.126 |

(Standard deviations omitted here; they are on the page and the largest is
MAPLE's 0.128 on GT-Shapley. The BF-SHAP column named in section 5.1 is not in
table 1.)

**Three readings, and the paper prints only the first.**

1. **The paper's own**, p. 7: \enquote{the relative performance of explainers
   varies dramatically across metrics}, and \enquote{while breakDown achieves the
   worst score for GT-Shapley, it achieves the best score for monotonicity}. It
   explains the second mechanically: breakDown greedily picks features by their
   effect on the output, with replacement, which is exactly what monotonicity
   checks. The section is headed *Performance across metrics* and its subject
   throughout is failure modes **of the explainers**.
2. **The book's first reading, and it is arithmetic on the paper's own cells.**
   On monotonicity the seven columns span 0.517 to 0.562, a range of 0.045, and
   **the RANDOM baseline at 0.525 is not last** - it beats LIME. On ROAR, RANDOM
   at 0.380 beats L2X and breakDown. On GT-Shapley the same seven span 0.004 to
   0.930 and the four methods that aim at Shapley values clear the baseline by
   more than half a point. So two of the five metrics barely separate a random
   attribution from published methods on this row, and one of the two ranks a
   random vector above a published method.
3. **The book's second reading, and it is the sharper one.** The paper's own
   explanation of breakDown's monotonicity win is that breakDown optimises, by
   construction, the quantity monotonicity measures. A metric that a method can be
   built to win by construction is not evidence about the property the metric is
   named for. That is chapter 08's diagnosis and paper 22's finding that
   evaluation metrics get chosen to confirm the contribution, arriving as a single
   printed row.

**Does this refute section 9.7? No, and the reason is precise.** GT-Shapley's
criterion is the exact Shapley value of a known distribution: a chosen
attribution *definition* computed without error, not a statement about what the
model's behaviour depends on. It is the right-hand side of definition 1.2 only if
one has already granted that the Shapley value is the right answer, which is the
assumption chapters 04 and 06 spend two chapters not granting. So the table
settles which method best approximates Shapley values, and it does not ask
whether any of the five metrics measures faithfulness. Section 9.7's sentence is
about that question, and it stands.

**What the chapter takes.** The gap is no longer only that nobody has run the
comparison. It is that **on the attribution side the comparison's raw material has
been published since 2021 in a table designed for a different purpose, and the
field's own meta-evaluation paper says two years later that the comparison cannot
be run.** One side declared it impossible; the other side, in a different
literature, constructed the ground truth and ran it. That is the shape of the
gap, and it is a shape no intersection of limitation statements produces.
