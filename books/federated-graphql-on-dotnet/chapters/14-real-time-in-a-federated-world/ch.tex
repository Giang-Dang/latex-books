\chapter{Real-Time in a Federated World}
\label{ch:real-time-in-a-federated-world}

Mosaic has had a subscription since
chapter~\ref{ch:schema-design-that-survives-change}. Nothing has sent one
through a router, and the reason is written into that chapter's own prose:
newman speaks request and response, a subscription is a connection that stays
open, and rather than build a second harness for one field I said plainly that
the two transcripts it printed were the only evidence nothing re-checked. Nine
chapters later the field is still there, still working, and still outside every
gate in this book.

So this chapter starts by finding out whether it works at all through the seven
subgraphs and the router in front of them. It does, immediately, with nothing
changed. That answer took about a minute and it is the least interesting thing
here.

What the rest of the chapter is about is the three questions underneath it.
What does a message cost, given that the service holding the connection cannot
answer half of what the client asked for. Which of the two WebSocket protocols
that share a name is this stack actually speaking, and who chose. And whether
the subgraph on the other end of that connection needs to exist, which turns
out to be a real question with a surprising answer.

By the end Mosaic has a second subscription that answers the same question as
the first and shares no machinery with it, and an eighth subgraph that is a
file. The write path is here too, because you cannot watch a subscription
without one and \code{submitReview} had never been through the router either.

Everything the composer says was said by wgc 0.129.7, which bundles version
0.63.2 of \code{@wundergraph/composition}. The router is Cosmo 0.337.1,
unchanged since chapter~\ref{ch:enter-the-router} except for one new block in
its configuration. Every line of library source is HotChocolate at tag
\code{16.6.0}, commit \code{8fea46e}. The broker is NATS 2.12.
