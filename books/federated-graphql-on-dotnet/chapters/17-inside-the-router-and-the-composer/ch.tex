\chapter{Inside the Router and the Composer}
\label{ch:inside-the-router-and-the-composer}

Chapter~\ref{ch:inside-hotchocolate} read an executor that somebody at
ChilliCream wrote in C\#. This chapter reads the two processes in front of it,
and neither of them is written in a language this book has used: the composer is
TypeScript and the router is Go. That turns out to matter less than the other
thing they have in common, which is that no Mosaic developer wrote a line of
either and both make decisions that show up in Mosaic's bills.

\index{router!and the composer, read as source}%
Four chapters have asked the same question in four different forms and none
could answer it. Chapter~\ref{ch:how-a-federated-query-actually-runs} asked
whether the plan for a query is cached between requests.
Chapter~\ref{ch:enter-the-router} asked whether two documents that plan to
identical JSON share a cache entry, and was careful to say it had not found out.
Chapter~\ref{ch:entity-resolution-done-right} asked whether any of the
representation machinery reaches the key.
Chapter~\ref{ch:hard-modeling-problems} asked about one argument on one field.
The answer is in section~\ref{sec:ch17-what-the-plan-cache-keys-on}, it is the
same answer to all four, and the reason nobody had it is worth stating first: a
Cosmo Router has metrics for seven of its caches and publishes none of them
unless you ask.

Two things I did not expect came out of measuring it. Eleven documents that
differ in their operation name, their variable names, their argument values and
their whitespace all share one plan, and then an alias buys a second one. And
the two headers that show you what the planner did are the two that guarantee it
was done, so the planning time in a trace is the cold number and there is no way
to see the warm one.

\index{composer!satisfiability, and which route it blames}%
On the composer's side this chapter pays a debt from
chapter~\ref{ch:composition}, which measured that an unresolvable key is
reported against exactly one of Catalog's four root fields returning a product
and said outright that it could not explain why. It reports the first route in
declaration order, and I know that because I moved a field to the top of
\code{type Query} and watched the blame follow it.

Four source trees, all cloned and read rather than browsed a page at a time.
The composer is \code{@wundergraph/composition} at tag \code{0.63.2}, commit
\code{818c67e}. The router is \code{router@0.337.1}, commit \code{2f50ab8}. The
planner is a third project, \code{graphql-go-tools} at \code{v2.14.2}, commit
\code{ed1d3ad}: it is what the router's \code{go.mod} pins, and it is where
almost everything interesting in this chapter turned out to live. Apollo appears
once, in section~\ref{sec:ch17-from-a-document-to-a-fetch-tree}, at
\code{@apollo/query-planner@2.14.3}.

The companion repository is at tag \code{ch17}, at which no Mosaic service
changes by a line. What the tag adds is two case scripts, a step in each
verification script for each of them, and one comment in
\code{router/config.yaml} about a default nobody should copy.
